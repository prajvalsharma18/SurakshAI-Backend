# SURAKSHAI Welfare Support Knowledge

This local prototype corpus provides general, non-disciplinary welfare-support guidance for the SURAKSHAI system. It is intended to support respectful human welfare conversations and grounded recommendation generation.

This corpus is **not** an organizational SOP, clinical protocol, emergency service, legal policy, personnel fitness assessment, or disciplinary framework. It does not establish mandatory actions, leave entitlements, duty limits, medical conclusions, or command decisions.

SURAKSHAI recommendations are decision-support information only. A model output must not be treated as a conclusion about a person's character, performance, health, fitness, or conduct. Where individualized, urgent, or specialized support is required, an appropriately qualified welfare, counselling, or health professional should determine the appropriate next step.

---

## Workload Management

A short review of recent duties can help identify unusually dense work periods, competing priorities, repeated high-demand tasks, unclear responsibilities, and limited recovery opportunities.

A welfare officer can use the discussion to understand whether the person is experiencing:

* unusually concentrated duties;
* repeated periods of high workload;
* difficulty separating urgent from non-urgent tasks;
* insufficient time for recovery between demanding assignments;
* competing responsibilities or unclear task priorities;
* a recent increase in workload compared with the person's normal pattern.

Practical support may include clarifying priorities, sequencing tasks, identifying work that can be deferred or redistributed through the appropriate process, and discussing whether additional recovery time should be considered.

A workload discussion should remain supportive. It should not be used to assign blame or infer poor performance.

The recommendation layer must not decide that a person is unable to perform their duties. It may identify that a workload review or welfare conversation could be useful.

---

## Recovery and Rest

Regular recovery opportunities can be considered alongside operational requirements.

A welfare discussion may review:

* whether adequate pauses are occurring between demanding activities;
* whether the person has had meaningful recovery opportunities;
* whether duty patterns have recently become more demanding;
* whether rest is being repeatedly interrupted;
* whether the person is able to maintain practical recovery routines.

General support ideas include protected breaks where permitted, realistic transition time between demanding duties, hydration, quiet recovery periods, and maintaining routines that support rest.

Recovery planning should consider the person's actual operational context. A recommendation must not assume that a particular schedule, break duration, or recovery arrangement is always possible.

The system should not infer a medical or psychological condition from workload or recovery data alone.

---

## Duty Scheduling and Night Duty

Frequent, consecutive, or closely spaced night duties may make recovery planning more important.

A welfare check-in can review:

* recent night-duty frequency;
* consecutive night duties;
* transitions between day and night schedules;
* opportunities for recovery after demanding duty periods;
* whether the person has concerns about the current pattern;
* whether the concern should be raised through the appropriate welfare or scheduling channel.

Possible support actions include discussing recovery needs, reviewing recent duty patterns with the appropriate human authority, and identifying whether additional welfare follow-up would be useful.

This corpus does not define mandatory roster limits, duty-hour limits, fitness standards, or scheduling entitlement rules.

The system should never convert a night-duty pattern directly into a disciplinary or fitness conclusion.

---

## Leave and Recovery Planning

Leave can be part of a broader conversation about recovery and welfare.

A welfare officer may discuss:

* whether the person has been able to use available recovery opportunities;
* whether a recent period of demanding duty makes recovery planning worth discussing;
* whether the person wants assistance navigating the appropriate internal process;
* whether additional welfare follow-up may be useful.

The recommendation layer may suggest reviewing leave or recovery planning, but it must not:

* promise leave approval;
* invent leave balances or entitlements;
* assume that leave will be granted;
* make a scheduling decision;
* represent an organizational policy that has not been provided to the system.

Any leave request, approval, denial, or scheduling decision remains subject to the organization's actual process and authorized human decision-makers.

## Sleep Support

General sleep-support guidance may be useful when a person reports difficulty maintaining adequate rest.

Where duties allow, practical support ideas include:

* maintaining a reasonably consistent rest routine;
* reducing avoidable stimulation before intended sleep;
* creating a rest environment that is as comfortable and suitable as practical;
* protecting available recovery periods;
* discussing how changing duty schedules may affect the person's ability to rest.

A welfare conversation should focus on the person's experience rather than assuming a cause.

Persistent, severe, or concerning sleep difficulties should be discussed with an appropriately qualified professional.

SURAKSHAI should not diagnose insomnia, sleep disorders, or other medical conditions.

Sleep-related recommendations are general support guidance and should not be interpreted as medical treatment.

## Fatigue and Recovery Check-ins

When recent duty patterns indicate sustained demands, a welfare officer may consider a brief check-in focused on recovery and support.

Useful discussion areas may include:

* how the person feels about their recent workload;
* whether recovery periods have been sufficient from their perspective;
* whether they are experiencing difficulty maintaining normal routines;
* whether they would like help identifying available support;
* whether follow-up is appropriate.

The purpose of the check-in is to understand support needs, not to interrogate or judge the person.

A model-generated risk category should be treated as a prompt for review rather than proof that the person is experiencing fatigue or another condition.

The person's own account remains important and should not be replaced by the model.

---

## Social and Welfare Check-ins

A private and respectful conversation with a trusted welfare contact can provide an opportunity to understand what support a person wants.

Good welfare conversations should:

* preserve dignity and confidentiality;
* use open and non-judgmental questions;
* allow the person to describe concerns in their own words;
* avoid assuming that a model output is correct;
* focus on support options rather than blame;
* respect the person's agency over voluntary support conversations.

A welfare officer may ask what kind of assistance would be useful, whether workload or recovery needs should be discussed, and whether the person would like information about available support pathways.

The system should not automatically interpret a lack of engagement as refusal, misconduct, or lack of concern.

---

## Counselling and Referral Pathways

A referral discussion may be appropriate when a person requests help, reports persistent difficulty, or indicates that additional support would be useful.

Possible actions include discussing access to an appropriately qualified welfare, counselling, or health professional through channels that are actually available in the organization.

The recommendation layer must not invent:

* programme names;
* hotline numbers;
* contact details;
* guaranteed appointment availability;
* legal obligations;
* organizational referral procedures that have not been supplied.

A referral recommendation should remain a support option rather than an automatic command.

The system should distinguish between offering information about a support pathway and requiring a person to use that pathway.

---

## Workload Review and Support Conversation

A welfare intervention can focus on understanding whether the person's recent operational pattern is creating avoidable pressure.

A structured conversation may cover:

1. Recent workload pattern.
2. Recent duty and recovery pattern.
3. Leave or recovery opportunities.
4. The person's own concerns.
5. Practical support options.
6. Whether follow-up is requested.
7. Whether another qualified professional should be involved.

The officer should avoid presenting the conversation as a performance review unless a separate authorized process requires one.

Welfare support should remain separate from disciplinary action.

---

## General Resilience Practices

Small, practical routines may support general wellbeing.

Examples include:

* planning priorities before a demanding work period;
* taking permitted breaks;
* maintaining a regular recovery routine where operationally possible;
* using brief relaxation or breathing exercises;
* maintaining supportive social contact;
* making time for practical recovery activities;
* reviewing workload before it becomes unnecessarily concentrated.

These practices are optional support ideas.

They are not a substitute for individualized professional advice and should not be presented as guaranteed solutions.

---

## Supportive Follow-up

A welfare check-in can be followed by a later review when the person and authorized welfare staff consider follow-up useful.

Follow-up may review:

* whether the person's concerns changed;
* whether an agreed welfare action was completed;
* whether additional support is requested;
* whether workload or recovery patterns remain concerning;
* whether referral or additional professional support should be discussed.

Follow-up should not be treated as surveillance.

The goal is continuity of support, not continuous monitoring of personal behaviour.

---

## Interpreting SURAKSHAI Outputs

SURAKSHAI risk categories and explanations are intended to support human review.

A risk category should be interpreted together with:

* the underlying operational context;
* available voluntary wellness information where consent permits its use;
* the person's own account;
* relevant welfare workflow information;
* limitations and missing-data indicators.

SHAP contributions describe how features influenced the model output. They are not proof that a particular factor caused the person's situation.

A model output should therefore be treated as:

**"A signal that may justify a welfare review"**

rather than:

**"A conclusion about the person."**

---

## Privacy and Dignity

Welfare information should be handled using the minimum information needed for the support task.

The system should avoid unnecessary exposure of:

* direct personal identifiers;
* personal contact details;
* private information unrelated to welfare support;
* raw sensitive data;
* unnecessary wellness detail.

Voluntary wellness information should only be accessed or processed where the appropriate consent permits it.

A person should not be disadvantaged merely because they choose not to provide voluntary wellness information.

Privacy protection is part of the welfare objective, not an optional technical feature.

---

## Human-in-the-Loop Principle

SURAKSHAI is designed to support human welfare decisions, not replace them.

Human welfare personnel remain responsible for:

* understanding context;
* discussing concerns respectfully;
* deciding whether intervention is appropriate;
* selecting an appropriate support pathway;
* determining whether follow-up is necessary;
* involving qualified professionals when needed.

The system must not independently issue disciplinary actions, medical diagnoses, fitness determinations, or mandatory welfare interventions.

---

## Data Limitations

Recommendations depend on the information available to the system.

Missing, incomplete, outdated, or voluntarily withheld information can affect the usefulness of recommendations.

A lack of wellness data does not mean that a person has no welfare concern.

Similarly, the presence of operational or wellness indicators does not establish that a person is experiencing a specific condition.

Where important information is missing, the system should prefer transparency over unsupported inference.

---

## Scope of This Knowledge Base

This corpus provides general welfare-support guidance for prototype recommendation and retrieval purposes.

It does not define:

* organizational policy;
* disciplinary procedures;
* emergency procedures;
* clinical treatment;
* medical diagnosis;
* legal obligations;
* service-specific duty limits;
* leave entitlement rules;
* mandatory intervention thresholds.

Where an actual organizational policy or professional protocol exists, that authoritative source should take precedence over this prototype knowledge base.
